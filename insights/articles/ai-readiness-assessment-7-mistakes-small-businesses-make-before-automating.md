<p>AI is already showing up in small businesses.</p>

<p>The question isn’t whether you can buy an AI tool. You can.</p>

<p>The better question is: <strong>Which part of your business is ready for AI, and what would responsible automation actually require?</strong></p>

<p>That’s what an AI readiness assessment should answer.</p>

<p>According to the <a href="https://resources.ipsos.com/rs/297-CXJ-795/images/Ipsos-AI-Monitor-2026.pdf">U.S. Chamber of Commerce Foundation and Ipsos Main Street AI Monitor</a>, published June 17, 2026, about half of workers at small businesses already use AI at work. But 64% of those users say their primary use is personal productivity (such as drafting, summarizing, or brainstorming) while only 6% use AI to automate workflows with minimal human involvement.</p>

<p>There’s a gap between using AI occasionally and building a dependable process around it.</p>

<p>That’s the gap this assessment is meant to clarify.</p>

<p>Before you ask how to use AI in a small business, look at how the work actually moves through your business. Here are seven common mistakes to avoid.</p>

<h2>1. Starting With the Tool Instead of the Process Creates Faster Confusion</h2>

<p>A new AI subscription can feel like progress.</p>

<p>You see a demonstration. The tool writes an email, summarizes a document, or generates a plan. It looks useful, so you sign up and start looking for somewhere to use it.</p>

<p>But where does it belong?</p>

<p>If you haven’t mapped the process first, you may automate a step that depends on missing information, unclear decisions, or inconsistent handoffs. The result isn’t a better workflow. It’s faster confusion.</p>

<p><strong>What to do instead:</strong></p>

<ul>

<li>Choose one repeated workflow.</li>

<li>Map what happens from beginning to end.</li>

<li>Identify each person, system, document, and decision involved.</li>

<li>Find the delay, rework, duplication, or manual entry.</li>

<li>Then evaluate whether AI fits.</li>

</ul>

<p>A process map doesn’t need to be complicated. A page of boxes and arrows can reveal more than a long list of possible AI tools.</p>

<p>Start with the work. Choose the technology second.</p>

<h2>2. Skipping the Baseline Means Comparing AI to a Feeling</h2>

<p>How will you know whether automation helped?</p>

<p>Many owners can say, “This takes too long,” or “My team spends a lot of time on this.” That’s a useful starting point, but it isn’t a baseline.</p>

<p>Without a baseline, you’re comparing AI to a feeling.</p>

<p>Before changing a process, record what you reasonably can:</p>

<ul>

<li>How many times the task occurs</li>

<li>How long it usually takes</li>

<li>How often work gets returned or corrected</li>

<li>Where errors occur</li>

<li>How long customers or prospects wait</li>

<li>Who reviews or approves the output</li>

</ul>

<p>You don’t need a perfect measurement system. You need a consistent point of comparison.</p>

<p>For example, if a generic service business processes a recurring set of client intake forms, it might track the number received each week, average handling time, correction requests, and time spent on review. Those numbers create something to compare after a change.</p>

<p>The point isn’t to promise a certain number of hours saved. The point is to make the result inspectable.</p>

<h2>3. Automating a Job or Role Treats Too Much Work as One Task</h2>

<p>“Can AI handle my inbox?”</p>

<p>“Can it do our marketing?”</p>

<p>“Can it manage client follow-up?”</p>

<p>These questions make sense. They also describe jobs, not tasks.</p>

<p>A job usually contains many different activities:</p>

<ol>

<li>Receive information</li>

<li>Sort or classify it</li>

<li>Check for missing details</li>

<li>Apply a rule</li>

<li>Make a judgment</li>

<li>Draft a response</li>

<li>Request approval</li>

<li>Update a system</li>

<li>Follow up later</li>

</ol>

<p>Some of those steps may be suitable for AI. Others may require human judgment, customer context, permissions, or professional responsibility.</p>

<p>Break the role into its component tasks before deciding what to automate. You may find that AI fits one narrow step rather than the entire function.</p>

<p>That’s not a limitation. It’s useful precision.</p>

<h2>4. Scoring Features Instead of the Task Leads to the Wrong Fit</h2>

<p>A tool may have dozens of impressive features.</p>

<p>That doesn’t mean it fits your process.</p>

<p>The better question is whether the <strong>task</strong> is a strong candidate for AI. A practical AI readiness assessment should look at criteria such as:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Repetitive:</strong> Does the task occur frequently?</li>

<li><strong>Digital or text-based:</strong> Is the necessary information already stored electronically?</li>

<li><strong>Rule-guided:</strong> Can you explain the normal decision rules?</li>

<li><strong>Meaningful volume:</strong> Does the task happen often enough to matter?</li>

<li><strong>Reviewable:</strong> Can a qualified person check the output before it creates risk?</li>

</ul>

<p>The more of these characteristics a task has, the stronger its case may be.</p>

<p>A task that is rare, highly personal, poorly documented, and difficult to review may not be ready. A repetitive, digital, rule-guided task with a clear reviewer may deserve a closer look.</p>

<p>This is where a task-based scoring matrix helps. Instead of asking, “What can this tool do?” you ask, “What does this task require, and where could AI safely assist?”</p>

<h2>5. Confusing a Wrench With a Worker Creates the Wrong Architecture</h2>

<p>A wrench is useful for one kind of work.</p>

<p>A worker can coordinate several steps, respond to changing conditions, and move a process forward.</p>

<p>The same distinction applies to AI.</p>

<p>A single-purpose AI tool, a “wrench”, may be the right choice for drafting meeting summaries, extracting information from forms, or checking documents against a defined list.</p>

<p>A coordinated AI agent, a digital “worker”, may make more sense when the workflow involves several connected steps, such as receiving a request, looking up information, preparing a draft, updating a record, and routing the work for approval.</p>

<p>Sometimes the reverse is true. A dedicated agent may be more complexity than you need. A focused tool may be easier to manage and review.</p>

<p>And sometimes the answer is neither yet.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://sbecouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/SBE-Technology-Use-Survey-March-2026-Final.pdf">SBE Council’s Small Business Technology Use Survey</a>, based on 517 small business employers surveyed February 17–23, 2026, reported that 82% had adopted at least one AI tool and that the median small business used five AI tools. The survey also reported median annual AI-tool spending of $2,200.</p>

<p>Multiple tools aren’t automatically a problem. But if each tool operates separately, you may create more handoffs, duplicated data, and review work.</p>

<p>The right question is not, “Should we use an agent?”</p>

<p>It is: <strong>What structure fits the workflow?</strong></p>

<h2>6. Counting Only the Subscription Hides the Full Cost</h2>

<p>A monthly subscription is easy to see.</p>

<p>It is also only one part of the cost.</p>

<p>A realistic assessment should consider:</p>

<ul>

<li>Process cleanup</li>

<li>Data preparation</li>

<li>Initial setup</li>

<li>System integration</li>

<li>Prompt or instruction design</li>

<li>Employee training</li>

<li>Human review</li>

<li>Error handling</li>

<li>Ongoing maintenance</li>

<li>Changes to the underlying software</li>

</ul>

<p>This does not mean every AI project will be expensive. It means you should calculate the full work required before deciding.</p>

<p><strong>Worked example:</strong> Suppose a small service business considers automating a recurring administrative workflow. The owner estimates that the tool would require 20 minutes of review per day, weekly maintenance, and initial setup by an internal employee. Those activities belong in the comparison alongside the subscription, not after the decision has already been made.</p>

<p>The goal is a clear financial case, not a dramatic promise.</p>

<p>What will the process require to operate reliably? What will it replace? What new work will it create? Those questions make the decision more honest.</p>

<h2>7. Skipping Rollback and Review Makes Automation Hard to Control</h2>

<p>What happens when the system makes a mistake?</p>

<p>If nobody has answered that question, the process isn’t ready for automation.</p>

<p>Before going live, define:</p>

<ul>

<li>Who owns the process</li>

<li>Who reviews the output</li>

<li>What requires approval</li>

<li>Which errors require escalation</li>

<li>What measurements will be checked</li>

<li>When the process will be reviewed</li>

<li>How the automation can be paused or turned off</li>

</ul>

<p>A rollback plan is not an admission that the project will fail. It is a basic control.</p>

<p>The Main Street AI Monitor found that 47% of small business workers cite privacy or security concerns as a barrier to AI use. The survey also found that 41% say it is unclear how AI applies to their specific business, while another 41% point to a skills gap. About one in ten respondents had been offered formal AI training.</p>

<p>Those concerns don’t disappear because a tool is easy to activate.</p>

<p>Clear ownership, review steps, training, and a way to stop the process help turn uncertainty into something manageable.</p>

<h2>An AI Readiness Assessment Should Give You Permission to Say “Not Yet”</h2>

<p>A useful assessment doesn’t begin with a predetermined technology recommendation.</p>

<p>It begins with your actual process.</p>

<p>We map how the work moves. We document the key steps. We identify where judgment, rules, data, and human review matter. Then we compare the realistic options.</p>

<p>The result may be:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Go:</strong> The process is clear, measurable, reviewable, and suitable for implementation.</li>

<li><strong>Go with caveats:</strong> AI may fit, but specific controls or cleanup are required first.</li>

<li><strong>Not yet:</strong> The process, data, ownership, or baseline needs more work.</li>

<li><strong>No:</strong> AI is not the right solution for this task.</li>

</ul>

<p>“No” is a legitimate result.</p>

<p>So is “not yet.”</p>

<p>The value of an AI readiness assessment is not that it gets you to automation. The value is that it helps you make the decision with confidence, without a pitch, without guesswork, and without buying technology just to prove you’re keeping up.</p>
